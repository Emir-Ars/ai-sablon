# How should an LLM-based coding agent turn research documents into a high-quality,
stable software project plan? I need measured evidence (experiments, benchmarks,
empirical studies), not opinion.

Questions:
1. Which planning practices measurably improve LLM agents' plan quality or later
   task success (plan-then-execute, task decomposition, specification-first,
   hierarchical planning)?
2. Does self-critique, reflection, or review by a different model improve LLM-generated
   plans? Is cross-model critique better than self-critique?
3. When LLMs extract requirements from long documents, what errors occur (hallucinated
   requirements, missed constraints, lost-in-the-middle), and which techniques reduce
   them (source citation, traceability, summarize-then-confirm)?
4. Over-reliance vs independent judgment: evidence on LLMs uncritically following
   flawed or outdated source documents, and methods that let them flag errors while
   staying transparent about what comes from the source vs their own reasoning.
5. Should an agent ask clarifying questions or proceed on assumptions? Evidence on
   clarification and task success.
6. Evidence on early risk reduction in software projects (spikes, prototyping,
   walking skeleton) and on how plan granularity affects later rework.

For each question: main findings, strength of evidence, sample/setting, year, and
limitations. Mark findings older than 12 months and anything not directly measured.

An LLM-based coding agent should clarify underspecified inputs before planning, generate a concise plan of no more than four steps using progressive or structured prompting, re-present that plan periodically during execution, and validate it through cross-model or execution-grounded critique rather than closed-loop self-review—while relying on externally supplied, high-quality plans wherever possible, since LLM-self-generated plans currently underperform even no-plan baselines in end-to-end evaluation.

## Abstract

Across ten empirical benchmark studies, the evidence consistently supports structured planning over unstructured generation, but with a critical caveat: plan quality determines whether planning helps or hurts. Well-formed plans improve issue-resolution rates across all tested LLM agents[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026], progressive prompting outperforms direct prompting with a large effect size (96.9% vs. 80.5%; Cohen's d = 1.63)[Jianru Shen et al., 2026], and a rollout-derived plan-then-re-execute cycle yields consistent if modest gains across model families[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025]. However, a subpar or misaligned plan is more damaging than no plan[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026], and LLM-generated plans underperformed even a no-plan baseline in end-to-end evaluation (33.33% vs. 42.75% win rate)[Varun Nathan et al., 2026]. On self-critique and reflection, the evidence is non-uniform: critique grounded in executed trajectories or structured cross-model handoffs improves outcomes[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025; Amine Barrak, 2025], while same-model self-critique is failure-type-dependent, succeeding on local logic errors but achieving 0% improvement on external integration failures[Jianru Shen et al., 2026], and adding explanation-plus-repair prompts to a code-conformance judge systematically lowered accuracy by amplifying false negatives[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. Separating comprehension from judgment, and removing mandatory repair steps, recovered accuracy in many conditions[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. Clarification-seeking before execution is beneficial: an uncertainty-aware multi-agent scaffold achieved a 69.40% resolve rate, significantly outperforming a standard single-agent baseline and approaching fully-specified-issue performance, with well-calibrated ask rates that tracked task complexity for one of the two tested backbones[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026]. Plan granularity matters measurably: plans exceeding four steps were consistently harder for all 14 tested LLMs to generate correctly[Varun Nathan et al., 2026], and adding task-relevant but internally misaligned phases degraded performance relative to a shorter standard plan[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026].

Several sub-questions remain empirically unaddressed by the available evidence. No included study directly measured hallucinated requirements, lost-in-the-middle constraint omission, or source-citation and summarize-then-confirm techniques during long-document extraction (Q3). Evidence on over-reliance on flawed source documents is indirect: agents follow subpar plans even to their detriment and cannot reliably self-correct without external grounding[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026], but no study deliberately introduced factually incorrect source documents and measured whether LLMs flagged the errors (Q4). Early risk-reduction practices such as spikes, walking skeletons, and prototyping were not evaluated in any included study, and evidence on plan granularity and later rework is limited to plan-length effects at generation time rather than downstream rework costs (Q6). Clarification-seeking results come exclusively from LLM-simulated users rather than real human interactions[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026], and the two 2025 studies on SAGE[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025] and code-conformance verification[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025] are older than twelve months. The practical synthesis across these findings is that an LLM-based coding agent should receive a high-quality, concise plan with periodic re-presentation rather than relying on self-generated or elaborately prompted plans; cross-model or grounded critique should be preferred over closed-loop self-critique; and underspecified inputs should trigger clarification before execution rather than silent assumption.

The studies span two broad task settings: software-repair agents evaluated on SWE-bench variants (Liu et al. 2026; Edwards & Schuster 2026; Hayashi et al. 2025; Jianru Shen et al. 2026) and requirement or specification generation evaluated against reference documents or structured quality rubrics (Sarim et al. 2025; Paiva et al. 2026; Jin & Chen 2025; Nathan et al. 2026; Lin et al. 2025; Barrak 2025). This divide is consequential for generalizability: the SWE-bench studies measure downstream task success using executable patch resolution as ground truth, while the specification-generation studies measure similarity to reference artifacts or intrinsic quality ratings. No study measures the full pipeline from document ingestion to confirmed software delivery.

The strongest evidence for the benefit of structured planning comes from Liu et al. (2026), who compared eight plan configurations across 21,120 trajectories and four LLMs, finding that the standard plan improved resolution uniformly[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. The progressive-prompting advantage reported by Shen et al. (2026) is the largest single effect (d = 1.63)[Jianru Shen et al., 2026], though it measures code completion against README requirements rather than against executable patch resolution, making direct comparison with SWE-bench studies difficult. Hayashi et al.'s SAGE cycle (2025) provides the only evidence for plan-then-re-execute as a distinct practice, with consistent but modest gains across model families[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025]. Critically, Nathan et al. (2026) found that LLM-generated plans were worse than no plan in end-to-end evaluation (33.33% vs. 42.75% win rate)[Varun Nathan et al., 2026], which converges with Liu et al.'s finding that a subpar plan is more damaging than no plan[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. The implication is that plan quality—not planning per se—is the operative variable.

Sarim et al. (2025) found that simpler prompting strategies (Zero-Shot, Few-Shot) matched or outperformed more elaborate ones on their HSSM metric[Mohammed Sarim et al., 2025], but because that metric measures textual similarity to reference task flows rather than downstream task execution, and because no human validation established that higher HSSM scores correspond to better plans[Mohammed Sarim et al., 2025], these results should not be interpreted as evidence that minimal prompting produces operationally superior plans.

The evidence on self-critique and reflection is notably non-uniform across study designs. Hayashi et al. (2025) found that SAGE's form of self-generated plan abstraction from one's own execution trace did improve resolution rates, but standard stepwise and episodic reflection did not[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025]. Shen et al. (2026) found that iterative same-model self-critique succeeded selectively for local, code-reviewable errors but completely failed on external integration failures[Jianru Shen et al., 2026]. Jin & Chen (2025) found that asking an LLM to explain and then repair its judgment of code conformance—a form of prompted self-critique—systematically lowered accuracy rather than improving it[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. Together these results suggest that the form and target of self-critique critically determines whether it helps.

Cross-model critique and structured multi-role pipelines show more consistently positive results. Barrak (2025) found substantial accuracy gains on PythonIO with the Planner→Executor→Critic accountable pipeline[Amine Barrak, 2025], and Shen et al. (2026) found that Multi-Model Collaboration achieved 99.2% mean completion, though at the cost of the longest average generation time (279 minutes)[Jianru Shen et al., 2026]. The Barrak study also quantified role-specific risk, finding that different LLMs carry different error profiles depending on whether they act as planner, executor, or critic[Amine Barrak, 2025]—a finding that implies heterogeneous pipeline composition (using different models in different roles) is not merely a design choice but can be Pareto-efficient[Amine Barrak, 2025].

Edwards & Schuster (2026) provide the most direct and methodologically specific evidence on this question. Their uncertainty-aware multi-agent scaffold (UA-MULTI) achieved a 69.40% overall resolve rate, significantly outperforming the standard single-agent baseline and approaching the performance of agents operating on fully specified issues[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026]. A structurally important finding is that tasks where the agent chose not to ask were resolved at a higher rate (76.92% and 75.38% for the two backbones) than tasks where it did ask (65.99% and 68.51%)[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026]. This asymmetry is most naturally explained by the agent correctly identifying hard tasks as needing clarification rather than by clarification itself being harmful on the asked tasks—though the simulated user context limits causal interpretation. The behavioral calibration result—that Claude Sonnet 4.5 showed stronger complexity sensitivity in ask rates than Kimi K2.6—points to an important model-specific variation in selectivity that is not captured by the overall resolve rate[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026].

Lin et al. (2025), assessed from abstract only, similarly found that an upstream requirement-clarification agent improved downstream code generation success compared to directly proceeding with the original description[Kaicun Lin et al., 2025], but the absence of quantitative effect sizes and benchmark identification limits the weight assignable to this finding.

Both studies share a critical limitation: neither involves real human users. Edwards & Schuster (2026) use an LLM-simulated user who answers exclusively from the original fully specified issue[Nicholas Edwards & Sebastian Schuster, 2026], and Lin et al. (2025) do not describe their user-response mechanism[Kaicun Lin et al., 2025]. The degree to which LLM-simulated responses capture the variability, ambiguity, and incompleteness of real human clarification responses is unknown.

# Thematic Analysis

## Q3: What Errors Occur When LLMs Extract Requirements from Documents, and Which Techniques Reduce Them?

Direct evidence on hallucinated requirements, missed constraints, or lost-in-the-middle failures during long-document extraction is limited across the included studies. The evidence that does exist comes primarily from two studies measuring different aspects of the problem.

Haolin Jin & Chen (2025) examined whether LLMs can reliably judge whether existing code satisfies natural-language specifications—a verification task that is structurally analogous to requirement extraction[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. Across more than 700 samples from HumanEval, MBPP, and QuixBugs, baseline RCRR was only 52%–78%, meaning that even on relatively short, well-scoped Python benchmarks, LLMs frequently misclassify correct implementations as noncompliant[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. The dominant error mode was false negatives—correct code judged erroneous—rather than false positives[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. Strikingly, more elaborate prompt engineering (adding explanation and repair steps) made this problem worse in many conditions[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. The Two-Phase Reflective and Behavioral Comparison prompts, which separated requirement comprehension from code auditing without mandating a corrective repair step, recovered accuracy in many model-dataset combinations, with GPT-4o on HumanEval reaching 85.4% under Behavioral Comparison versus 52.4% under the original prompt[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. This finding suggests that conflating comprehension, judgment, and correction into a single prompt conflates distinct cognitive operations in ways that introduce rather than reduce error.

Paiva et al. (2026) provide a complementary perspective on the upstream step: generating requirements from informal inputs (feature-request titles) rather than verifying code against requirements[G. Paiva et al., 2026]. They found that requirement quality—measured on Unambiguity, Verifiability, and Singularity—was strongly modulated by input clarity; vague or broad titles consistently produced requirements with low verifiability[G. Paiva et al., 2026]. This suggests that the error budget in a document-to-plan pipeline is partly determined before the LLM begins: if source documents are ambiguous, LLM-generated requirements will reflect that ambiguity regardless of prompt strategy[G. Paiva et al., 2026]. Prompt effects were also model-dependent: few-shot prompting improved Singularity for both models tested, but Expert Identity prompting sometimes raised Verifiability while simultaneously reducing Singularity—a quality attribute trade-off rather than a uniform improvement[G. Paiva et al., 2026].

No included study directly measured hallucinated requirements (requirements appearing in LLM output with no basis in the source document) or lost-in-the-middle effects (information from the middle of long documents being systematically underrepresented). The Nathan et al. (2026) contact-center planning study found persistent gaps in tool-prompt alignment and tool-usage completeness across all 14 tested LLMs[Varun Nathan et al., 2026], which could reflect a form of constraint omission, but the study measures plan quality against structured query decomposition rather than against a long research document, limiting the applicability of this finding to the research question as posed.

## Q4: Over-Reliance on Flawed Source Documents, and Methods for Transparent Flagging

Liu et al. (2026) provide the most direct empirical evidence relevant to this question, though framed as plan compliance rather than document over-reliance[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. Their key finding is that agents without an explicit plan fall back on internalized workflows from training, which are described as often incomplete, overfit, or inconsistently applied[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. Conversely, providing a subpar or task-misaligned plan was more harmful than providing no plan at all[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. This creates an operationally important asymmetry: agents cannot be expected to exercise independent judgment when no plan is given, but will also not reliably detect and correct a flawed plan when one is given—they tend to follow it even to their detriment[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026].

Liu et al. (2026) further found that inserting task-relevant phases that do not align with the model's internal problem-solving strategy degraded performance, particularly when added early in the plan sequence[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. This is consistent with an over-reliance interpretation: the agent applied the externally imposed ordering even when it conflicted with a more effective internal strategy. The paper's conclusion—that fine-tuning should teach models to follow instructed plans adaptively rather than encoding task-specific workflows—implies that current models are brittle in the direction of uncritical compliance rather than the direction of uncritical independence[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026].

Barrak (2025) found that a structured accountable pipeline, in which each agent's output is explicitly logged and compared against a gold reference at each handoff stage, substantially reduced error propagation relative to simple pipelines[Amine Barrak, 2025]. However, the accountability mechanism in that study required access to gold-standard labels to assign blame[Amine Barrak, 2025], which is not available in realistic planning-from-documents scenarios. The principle—that explicit traceability of which agent produced which output at which stage limits silent error propagation—is operationally generalizable even if the specific blame-assignment mechanism is not.

Neither citation-grounding, source-attribution prompting, nor summarize-then-confirm strategies were directly evaluated in any included study. The evidence on over-reliance is therefore indirect: it comes from studies of plan compliance in coding agents rather than from studies that deliberately introduced factually incorrect or outdated source documents and measured whether LLMs flagged the errors.

## Q6: Early Risk Reduction and Plan Granularity Effects on Later Rework

No included study directly evaluated spikes, walking-skeleton prototyping, or structured early-risk-reduction practices as experimental interventions. Evidence bearing on plan granularity—the closest available proxy—comes from two studies.

Nathan et al. (2026) found a consistent negative relationship between plan length and quality: plans exceeding four steps were substantially harder for all 14 tested LLMs to generate correctly[Varun Nathan et al., 2026]. The best total metric score across all models (84.8/100, Claude-3-7-Sonnet) was achieved on the same benchmark where step-length penalties were most visible[Varun Nathan et al., 2026]. Lineage prompting—providing ordered plan revisions as context—improved step executability for 9 of 14 models but had mixed effects on overall quality, with 6 models improving, 5 declining, and 3 unchanged on the A+ quality tier[Varun Nathan et al., 2026]. This suggests that iterative plan refinement can help with step-level quality without reliably improving global plan coherence.

Liu et al. (2026) found that augmenting the standard plan with additional phases—specifically regression-test execution before the navigation phase and a change-summary phase before submission—degraded issue-resolution performance rather than improving it[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]. The authors interpret this as a misalignment between externally imposed structure and the model's internal problem-solving strategy. The implication for granularity is that adding more steps to a plan is not uniformly beneficial and can introduce friction when the additional steps conflict with trained workflows.

# Synthesis

## Reconciling Conflicting Evidence on Critique and Reflection

The most pronounced apparent contradiction in the included evidence concerns whether self-critique and reflection improve LLM plan quality. Hayashi et al. (2025) found that SAGE's self-abstraction from an execution trace improved resolution rates[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025]; Lin et al. (2025) found that multi-agent clarification and conflict detection improved code generation[Kaicun Lin et al., 2025]; Barrak (2025) found that Planner→Executor→Critic pipelines improved accuracy substantially in some conditions[Amine Barrak, 2025]. Yet Jin & Chen (2025) found that adding explanation and repair prompts to a code-conformance judge reduced accuracy in many conditions[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025], and Hayashi et al. (2025) themselves found that standard stepwise and episodic reflection did not improve performance[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025].

The resolution lies in what information the critique agent or prompt has access to and what it is asked to do with it. In SAGE, the abstraction is grounded in the agent's own executed trajectory—a concrete, grounded experience record[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025]. In the Barrak pipeline, each agent receives the outputs of prior agents as structured, validated handoffs[Amine Barrak, 2025]. In the Lin et al. framework, the clarification agents have access to the full problem description before any code is generated[Kaicun Lin et al., 2025]. In contrast, in Jin & Chen's experiments, asking a model to explain its code-conformance judgment and then propose a repair caused it to generate increasingly elaborate rationalizations that amplified false-negative errors rather than correcting them[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025]. The distinction is between critique grounded in execution evidence or structured prior outputs versus critique that generates and then follows its own elaborations in a closed loop. The Two-Phase Reflective and Behavioral Comparison prompts in Jin & Chen (2025) improved performance precisely by breaking this loop—separating comprehension from judgment and removing the mandatory repair step[Haolin Jin & Huaming Chen, 2025].

Shen et al. (2026) add a further dimension: same-model self-critique is highly failure-type-dependent, succeeding on local logic errors and failing completely on external integration failures (0% improvement)[Jianru Shen et al., 2026]. Cross-model collaboration (GPT-5 analyzing, Claude implementing) achieved the highest completion rate but also the highest latency and cost[Jianru Shen et al., 2026]. RAG-Assisted achieved comparable completion with better efficiency specifically because retrieval provides grounding from outside the model's own representations[Jianru Shen et al., 2026]. These findings are consistent with the general principle that critique improves planning when it introduces information not already present in the generating model's context, and is unreliable or counterproductive when it only asks the model to reconsider its own output without additional grounding.

## Reconciling Conflicting Evidence on Plan Provision and Task Success

Liu et al. (2026) and Nathan et al. (2026) both found that low-quality plans can be worse than no plan. Liu et al. found that a subpar or misaligned plan hurt more than having no plan at all[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026]; Nathan et al. found that LLM-generated plans underperformed even the no-plan baseline in end-to-end answer quality (33.33% vs. 42.75% win rate)[Varun Nathan et al., 2026]. Yet Liu et al. also found that the standard (well-formed) plan consistently improved issue resolution across all models[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026], Hayashi et al. found that SAGE's plan-augmented execution consistently improved on its no-plan baseline[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025], and Shen et al. found that progressive prompting (a form of structured incremental planning) strongly outperformed direct prompting[Jianru Shen et al., 2026].

These findings are not contradictory: they define a quality threshold below which plan provision is harmful and above which it is beneficial. The operationally critical question for an LLM-based coding agent is therefore not "should I use a plan?" but "is this plan likely to be of sufficient quality to be beneficial?" Nathan et al.'s finding that human-reference plans (58.7% win rate) substantially outperform LLM-generated plans (33.33% win rate)[Varun Nathan et al., 2026] establishes that LLMs cannot yet reliably self-generate plans that surpass the no-plan baseline in complex, tool-grounded settings. Liu et al.'s finding that periodic plan reminders mitigate plan violations and improve success[Shuyang Liu et al., 2026] provides one operational lever for improving the utility of plans once they exist: re-anchoring the agent to the plan's structure at regular intervals rather than relying on a single upfront injection. Both findings together support a design principle: provide a high-quality plan with periodic re-presentation, and do not rely on the LLM to self-generate the plan during the same execution in which it will follow it—a separation that SAGE achieves through its two-stage rollout design[Hiroaki Hayashi et al., 2025].
